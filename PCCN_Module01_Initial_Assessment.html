02

Why Children Are Different

Lecture: “Why Children Are Different”

▶
Watch: Why Children Are Different~10 MIN · LIFETIME ACCESS

Children are not small adults

Respiratory

↓ Narrow airway ↓ Low reserve ↓ Low FRC ↑ High VO₂ ↑ Big dead space

→ early hypoxia, fast.

Circulatory

CO = rate-dependent Low SVR Low blood volume Cools down fast Low glycogen

→ hidden shock, underestimated losses.

Thermoregulation & metabolic reserve restored

↑ Fast heat loss — high surface area : body weight ↓ Low glycogen stores — faster hypoglycaemia in illness

Reserve at a glance — why the margin is thinner in children

Respiratory reserve
Thin — tips into hypoxia fast
Circulatory reserve
Thin — shock hides, then decompensates fast
Metabolic reserve
Thin — heat and glucose drop quickly
Illustrative, not to clinical scale — the point is: children start with less margin across every system.
Vitals are the tip of the iceberg — compensation hides deterioration until it's almost too late. HR, BP, SpO₂, GCS can all look fine right up to collapse. Early read beats a late number.

Spot the pattern — 3 kids, same day

3 months

Fever, fast breathing, chest indrawing, poor feed, drowsy

5 years

Vomiting, pain abdomen, dehydrated, drowsy

1 year

Fever, cough, poor feed, unresponsive

Same “viral illness” story on the surface — each hides a red flag a rushed look would miss.

Minimum acceptable blood pressure, by age expanded

The quick formula in the next section (“70 + 2×age”) only covers ages 1–10. Here is the full age band.

AgeMinimum acceptable systolic BP
Term neonate (0–28 days)< 60 mmHg
Infant (1–12 months)< 70 mmHg
Children 1–10 years< 70 + (age × 2) mmHg
Children > 10 years< 90 mmHg
Easy mnemonic: 60 – 70 – 80 – 90 is the rough minimum systolic BP walking up through neonate → infant → toddler → older child.
Source: PALS 2021 Provider Handbook (Karl Disque), as cited in the PEERS Nurse Manual.
Quick check — Why do children deteriorate faster than adults in respiratory illness?
Correct — low FRC + high VO₂ + narrow airway = fast tip into hypoxia.
Not quite — look at the Respiratory chips above.

The DIRECT Approach

Lecture: “The DIRECT Approach”

▶
Watch: The DIRECT Approach~14 MIN · LIFETIME ACCESS
A cycle, not a checklist — keeps running from first contact to safe transfer.
70% of serious errors trace back to broken communication, not missing knowledge — that's why Effective Communication is its own pillar.

Reassessment = EII cycle

Evaluate → Identify → Intervene, on repeat. Document every 5 min if unstable, 15 min if stable.

PEWS — catch the ward slide

BehaviourCardiovascularRespiratory

Score 0–3 each. Rising score (2→5) → senior review, rapid response, consider PICU — before collapse.

Secondary — SAMPLE

Signs/symptomsAllergiesMedicationsPast historyLast mealEvents
Unexplained deterioration? Run the reversible causes — H's: hypovolaemia, hypoxia, H⁺/acidosis, hypo/hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia. T's: tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis.
Quick check — A ward nurse notices a child's PEWS has risen from 2 to 5. What should happen?
Correct — a PEWS jump like this needs immediate escalation.
Not quite — a rising PEWS should trigger immediate escalation.

Airway/circulation tools on top — fastest need, fastest reach.
Daily: seals + expiry checked During code: 1 nurse runs the cart Post-code: restock immediately
36% fewer complications, 47% fewer deaths — that's what a simple checklist did in the WHO's 8-hospital trial. Same logic for a PICU: a checked cart + standard handover is what lets a team perform under pressure. (Case in point: all 155 aboard the Hudson River ditching survived — preparation, not luck.)

Prepared units don't get lucky — they get ready

Run this check at the start of every shift, before every procedure.

When it's bigger than one child — disaster triage

Mass-casualty and pandemic surge triage flips the goal: from saving this child to saving the most children with the resources on hand. START/JumpSTART sorts by colour, in seconds, no equipment:

REDImmediate
YELLOWDelayed
GREENMinor
BLACKExpectant
Red: life-threatening, treat now Yellow: serious but stable Green: walking wounded Black: non-survivable given resources
Same ethics every time: Justice (fair allocation), Beneficence (maximise survival), Non-maleficence (avoid harm), Transparency (open decisions).

Hospital preparedness checklist

✓ Dedicated triage area ✓ PEWS charting ✓ Mock drills (quarterly) ✓ Communication lines ✓ Equipment maintenance ✓ Disaster plan integration

Without it, this happens

Bronchiolitis, admitted 5pm, looking well.

9pm: “not looking well” — reassured, suctioned
→
9:30pm: HR 220 — doctor “busy”
→
10:05pm: SpO₂ 88% — CODE BLUE — arrest

The nurse checked on this child four times. Every visit was a chance to escalate with a structured score, not a gut feeling. None did — because there was no system making escalation the easy, obvious choice. That's what preparedness fixes.
